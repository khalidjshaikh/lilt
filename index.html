The language

A sentence is
a small song.

Lilt is a one-way acoustic modem with the manners of a language. It uses sixteen pure tones between 600 and 2100 Hz. Each tone carries one nibble; two nibbles make one byte of UTF-8 text.

Nothing is sent over Wi-Fi or Bluetooth. The speaker pushes pressure into the room, and the microphone reads it back.

00 Call two-tone chord · 600 ms
→
01 Greeting eight known syllables
→
02 Head version · id · length · checksum
→
03 Body UTF-8 text + Reed–Solomon

The sixteen syllables

Tones are spaced 100 Hz apart. The receiver compares their energy forty times a second; a tiny clock inside the greeting keeps every syllable aligned.

Error correction

Every eleven syllables of data gain four parity syllables. The resulting RS(15, 11) codeword can repair two wrong tones. Body blocks are interleaved, so a cough or a chair scrape is spread across several codewords instead of destroying one.

Then a checksum

A CRC-16 seals the message. The receiver only shows text after the greeting, head, body and seal all agree. If the air was too noisy, it says so rather than inventing words.

Why so slow?

A room is a messy channel: echoes, rattles, fan noise. Lilt spends its bandwidth on being understood rather than being fast. It carries about 59 bits per second after error correction — enough for a sentence, not a stream.
